\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Motivation}
The classical guitar is taught, almost universally, through graded repertoire: a student progresses through pieces of increasing technical demand, and the teacher's central curricular task is to select works that lie within the student's current reach while still advancing their technique. This selection shapes lesson planning, the order in which techniques are introduced, how efficiently practice time is spent, and, not least, whether the student remains motivated or becomes discouraged by a piece encountered too early. The difficulty judgments underlying these decisions are made constantly in guitar pedagogy, yet they rest almost entirely on individual teacher experience and informal consensus. Two experienced teachers may disagree about the same piece, and neither can appeal to a shared, reproducible standard to settle the question.

Graded repertoire databases partially address this need. GuitarBurst~\cite{guitarburst}, for example, assigns classical guitar works an integer difficulty grade from 1 to 20, and the existence and popularity of such resources demonstrates a genuine demand for structured difficulty information. However, these grades are assigned manually, piece by piece, and the databases offer no account of \emph{why} a piece received its grade --- which technical properties of the score pushed it to level 12 rather than level 8. Manual grading also does not scale: the volume of available repertoire, particularly in public-domain score archives, far exceeds what any curated database covers.

At the same time, research in music information retrieval (MIR) has shown that symbolic score representations, such as MusicXML and MIDI, carry sufficient signal for automatic difficulty estimation. Yaroch~\cite{yaroch2025measure} estimated piano score difficulty from symbolic features extracted from MusicXML, and Ramoneda et al.~\cite{ramoneda2024rubricnet} demonstrated that piano difficulty can be predicted by an interpretable model built from a small set of hand-crafted descriptors, organized so that the model's output reads like a pedagogical rubric: each descriptor receives an independent score, and the scores are summed into the final difficulty estimate. Interpretability is not an incidental property in this setting. A difficulty estimate is only actionable in a pedagogical context if the teacher can inspect where it comes from and verify the reasoning against the score.

Nearly all of this work, however, targets the piano. The descriptors of RubricNet, the strongest interpretable model to date, are keyboard-specific: they assume separate staves per hand, key-span measurements, and piano fingering conventions, none of which transfer to the guitar, where a single staff encodes the work of both hands and the same pitch can be realized at several positions on the fretboard. On the guitar side, V{\'a}squez et al.~\cite{vasquez2023quantifying} formalized the playability of strummed chord progressions in pop music through descriptors such as barre usage and chord-transition difficulty, but solo classical repertoire --- with its contrapuntal textures, position shifts along the neck, and intricate right-hand arpeggiation --- has no comparable interpretable difficulty model.

This thesis addresses that gap. We adapt the RubricNet framework to classical solo guitar: the descriptor layer is redesigned around the instrument's actual technical demands, a dataset pairing symbolic scores with professional difficulty grades is assembled from scratch, and the resulting model is evaluated both on predictive performance and on whether its explanations align with how guitarists reason about difficulty. The aim is not merely a classifier that assigns a level, but one that can justify the level in terms a teacher would recognize --- stretch, shifting, barre load, texture --- and do so from the score alone.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem_statement}
Transferring interpretable difficulty estimation from the piano to the classical guitar poses several challenges that go beyond substituting one instrument's features for another's.

The first challenge concerns data. No existing resource pairs high-quality symbolic classical guitar scores with professional difficulty grades at a scale suitable for supervised learning. Graded databases such as GuitarBurst provide labels but no machine-readable scores; symbolic corpora such as DadaGP~\cite{sarmento2021dadagp} and GAPS~\cite{riley2024gaps} provide scores but no difficulty grades. A dataset must therefore be constructed by matching heterogeneous score sources against an external grading resource, which raises problems of title and composer ambiguity, cross-source duplication, and inconsistent file quality. The difficulty is compounded by the largest available score source, PDF tablature: parsing these vector-graphic documents recovers pitch, string, and fret reliably, but note durations are lost entirely. Without rhythm, any descriptor involving timing --- how fast a passage must be executed, how much time a position shift has available --- cannot be computed, even though execution speed is a central determinant of physical difficulty.

The second challenge concerns descriptor design under an interpretability constraint. The additive architecture that makes RubricNet transparent is also its principal restriction: no descriptor may interact with any other before the final summation, so the model cannot learn on its own that, for instance, a wide left-hand stretch is more demanding when it must be executed quickly. If such an interaction matters --- and in guitar technique it plainly does, since a five-fret jump during a fermata is trivial while the same jump inside a sixteenth-note run is not --- it must be encoded into a descriptor by hand. Conversely, uninformative descriptors are not merely useless to an additive model: because every input is assigned a score and summed into the output, a dead descriptor injects noise directly into the prediction. Descriptor engineering, guided by systematic auditing, is therefore where the principal modeling effort of this work lies.

The third challenge concerns the labels themselves. Difficulty grades are ordinal --- level 12 is harder than level 8, and misclassifying a piece by one level is a far smaller error than misclassifying it by four --- so both the model and the evaluation must respect the ordering rather than treat levels as unrelated categories. Moreover, the raw 1--20 scale is heavily imbalanced: intermediate levels are well populated while the most advanced levels contain only a handful of pieces each, which makes direct 20-way classification statistically untenable at the achievable dataset size and forces a principled coarsening of the label space. Finally, all grades originate from a single database, effectively a single grading tradition, so any evaluation is necessarily relative to that standard.

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research_questions}
The central objective of this thesis is to develop and validate an interpretable difficulty estimation model for classical guitar. The following research questions guide the investigation:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Which guitar-specific symbolic descriptors best capture the technical difficulty of classical guitar pieces, as reflected in graded repertoire resources such as GuitarBurst?}\\
    The first question addresses the descriptor layer: which measurable properties of a symbolic guitar score --- left-hand stretches, position shifts, barre chords, right-hand string crossings, texture, length --- actually carry signal about the grades assigned by an established grading resource, and which intuitively plausible properties do not.
    \item \textbf{Can an interpretable, rubric-based model using these hand-crafted descriptors predict piece-level classical guitar difficulty with useful accuracy?}\\
    The second question concerns the cost of interpretability: whether a strictly additive model, restricted to scoring each descriptor independently, can remain competitive with unconstrained black-box baselines that are free to exploit arbitrary feature interactions.
    \item \textbf{Does combining multiple dimensions of difficulty --- left-hand technique, right-hand technique, and global score complexity --- improve prediction over any single dimension alone?}\\
    The third question tests whether guitar difficulty is genuinely multi-dimensional. If a single aspect of technique sufficed to grade a piece, the multi-dimensional descriptor design would be unnecessary complexity.
    \item \textbf{To what extent do the model's descriptor contributions align with common pedagogical intuitions about what makes a classical guitar piece difficult?}\\
    The final question examines whether the model's interpretability is substantive rather than merely architectural: whether the learned per-descriptor contributions behave consistently across difficulty levels and agree with how teachers reason about technical demand.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Goals}
To address the challenges identified in the research questions, this thesis pursues the following goals:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Construct a labeled symbolic dataset for classical guitar difficulty.}\\
    Assemble a corpus pairing symbolic scores from heterogeneous sources with professional 1--20 difficulty grades, including a rhythm-recovery pipeline that restores note durations to PDF-derived scores by aligning them against matched MIDI performances.
    \item \textbf{Define a compact set of guitar-specific, interpretable descriptors.}\\
    Develop descriptors computable directly from symbolic notation, organized into left-hand, right-hand, and global dimensions, and audit them systematically so that only informative inputs reach the additive model.
    \item \textbf{Build and benchmark an interpretable difficulty classifier.}\\
    Adapt the additive RubricNet architecture to the guitar descriptor set and compare it against standard baselines under a fixed ordinal evaluation protocol, testing in particular whether the multi-dimensional design outperforms each dimension in isolation.
    \item \textbf{Validate the model's explanations.}\\
    Demonstrate that the trained model's per-descriptor contributions are pedagogically meaningful, by analyzing their influence, their monotonicity across difficulty levels, and their agreement with independent measures of feature importance.
\end{enumerate}

A substantial part of the work, implicit in the first goal but unavoidable in practice, is data engineering: since no suitable dataset existed, the dataset itself --- including the rhythm-alignment pipeline --- constitutes a contribution of this thesis.

\section{Thesis Structure}
In Chapter~\ref{ch:related_work}, we review prior work on symbolic difficulty estimation, multi-dimensional difficulty modeling, the RubricNet architecture, and guitar-specific playability metrics. In Chapter~\ref{ch:preliminaries}, we introduce the necessary preliminaries: the technical vocabulary of classical guitar playing, symbolic music representations, global sequence alignment, ordinal classification, and the baseline models used for comparison. In Chapter~\ref{ch:method}, we describe our method, including the construction of the dataset, the rhythm-alignment pipeline, the three generations of descriptor sets, and the model architecture together with its training procedure. In Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}, we evaluate the approach quantitatively against baseline models and qualitatively through an analysis of the trained model's internals, and report a series of auxiliary experiments. In Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}, we discuss the findings with respect to the research questions and the limitations of the approach. Finally, in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}, we summarize our contributions and outline directions for future work.

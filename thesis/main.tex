\chapter*{Abstract}
Matching repertoire to a student's technical level is a routine task in classical guitar teaching, yet the difficulty grades in repertoire databases are assigned manually and come with no explanation of why a piece earned its grade. Automatic difficulty estimation from symbolic scores has shown that interpretable prediction is possible, but existing work targets the piano, and its descriptors do not transfer to the guitar, where one staff encodes both hands and the same pitch can be played at several fretboard positions.

This thesis adapts RubricNet, an interpretable additive neural model, from piano to classical guitar. The model scores 32 hand-crafted descriptors independently and sums them, so every prediction decomposes into per-descriptor contributions like a grading rubric. The descriptors span left-hand demands (stretches, shifts, barre chords), right-hand demands (string crossing, plucking patterns), and global score properties (length, polyphony, note density). We assemble a dataset of 716 pieces, pairing symbolic scores from three sources with 1--20 grades from the GuitarBurst database; because PDF-derived scores lack rhythm, a Needleman--Wunsch alignment transplants note timing from matched MIDI performances, giving 89\% of the dataset usable rhythm information and enabling speed- and passage-aware descriptors.

On an 8-class ordinal task, the model reaches 31.0\% exact accuracy. A Random Forest baseline edges it on raw accuracy (33.1\%) and performs about the same on balanced accuracy ($\sim$0.29), but the interpretable model wins on the metrics that matter for ordinal prediction --- lower mean absolute error (1.069 vs.\ 1.122) and higher rank correlation (Kendall's $\tau$ 0.634 vs.\ 0.599) --- so when it errs, it errs by less. Two fuzzy rule-based classifiers, adapted from music genre classification as a second interpretable reference point, trail far behind on the same descriptors (Kendall's $\tau$ at most 0.48): the model's standing comes not from interpretability being cheap, but from combining it with an ordinal design. Accuracy rises to 67.6\% on a three-level easy/medium/hard scale. Per-descriptor contributions increase monotonically with difficulty and agree with Random Forest importances, supporting the trained model as a difficulty rubric grounded in guitar technique rather than an opaque predictor.
